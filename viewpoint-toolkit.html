Building Agentic Systems, Not Just AgentsNew

Data strategist Suzannah Hicks argues that most agent failures come from treating agents like prompts instead of systems — and that agentic AI needs an operating model and governance built in from the start, not a longer runtime prompt bolted on after the fact.

Key Insights

  • Her core distinction: "A prompt is designed to produce an output. An agent is designed to operate within a workflow. A multi-agent system is designed to move work across steps, roles, dependencies, decision points, and handoffs" — treating these as the same design problem is where most agent-building goes wrong
  • Before writing any runtime prompt, she starts with system-level questions: what's the agent's purpose and role, what's in and out of scope, what information should it rely on, what output standard must it meet, and where must human judgment remain
  • In multi-agent workflows, one agent's output becomes the next agent's input — so an unclear role or inconsistent output in an upstream agent can cause the whole system to drift, not just that one step
  • She uses knowledge sources as "governance artifacts," not just reference libraries: documents attached to an agent should define its purpose, boundaries, behavior rules, evidence expectations, and escalation points, not just give it facts to look up
  • Her diagnostic method for a failing agent: isolate which layer actually broke — role, source material, output standard, handoff design, or runtime prompt — rather than defaulting to a longer prompt

How to Debug a Team That Isn’t Working: The Waterline ModelNew

Molly Graham lays out the Waterline Model — a four-layer diagnostic (Structure, Dynamics, Interpersonal, Individual) for figuring out where a team problem is actually coming from before deciding what to do about it. Her rule of thumb, "snorkel before you scuba," argues that leaders default to blaming people when the real problem is almost always further up the stack.

Key Insights

  • The four layers, in diagnostic order: Structure (vision, goals, roles, expectations, org design) → Dynamics (how decisions, conflict, and information actually move day-to-day) → Interpersonal (trust and unresolved conflict between specific people) → Individual (skill gaps, stress, personal circumstances)
  • "Snorkel before you scuba" — always check the layers that affect the whole team first; going straight to individual diagnosis before ruling out structure and dynamics is the most common leadership trap
  • Real example: taking over a struggling marketing team, Graham asked each person to describe their role, their goals, and the metrics they owned — the answers were "wildly inconsistent," revealing a structural problem that made it impossible to fairly judge any individual until it was fixed
  • Dynamics example: a founder who says he wants speed but then reverses decisions and second-guesses people's judgment teaches the team to over-escalate and slow down — "optimizing for not being wrong instead of progress" is a rational team response to an unstable system, not a performance problem
  • Interpersonal conflict is often "a structural problem wearing a human face" — unclear roles or overlapping ownership put two people in each other's way — but real trust/style breakdowns can independently wreck a team even when structure and dynamics are sound
  • Individual-level diagnosis should only happen last, after structure and dynamics are ruled out — at that point it stops being diagnostic and becomes a decision: coach, change the role, or make a clean exit

First Round Review · Molly Graham · Sep 2015 · 13 min read

‘Give Away Your Legos’ and Other Commandments for Scaling StartupsNew

Molly Graham's classic "Give Away Your Legos" talk explains why scaling a team forces everyone to repeatedly hand off the work they built and go find something bigger — and why fighting that instinct is one of the most common ways high performers stall out. She maps the emotional and structural character of four distinct growth phases, from 30 employees to 750+.

Key Insights

  • The "Give Away Your Legos" metaphor: handing off part of your job as a company scales feels like a kid being asked to share Legos — natural anxiety that the new hire won't build the tower "right," or will take the best pieces — but holding on blocks your own growth: "if you hold on to answering customer support queries yourself... you're never going to run customer support or product marketing"
  • Concrete leadership move for someone who won't let go of a project: give them a much bigger goal instead of just asking them to release it — "we need you to do the same thing over here only five times bigger" — which resolves the anxiety by redirecting it rather than fighting it
  • Tell-tale signs a team needs this conversation: questions like "why did we hire that person?", "do we really need someone to do that job?", or "is Suzie going to take over this project?"
  • The Phases of Scale: 30–50 employees (tipping point from "family" to "company" — start writing down mission, values, and philosophies, and over-communicate them); 50–200 (the foundational culture-setting window — "hiring is a network effect: the first 100 people you hire will define the next 200," and the critical window to fire low performers quickly); 200–750 (culture is largely set — bad habits must be aggressively managed or they become permanent company DNA); 750+ (identity shifts from company to team, and politics — defined as people acting in self-interest rather than company interest — begins to emerge)
  • Author credibility: Graham's team at Google grew from 25 to 125 in 9 months; during her 4+ years at Facebook the company grew from 500 employees/80M users to 5,500 employees/1.1B users; she later became COO of Quip

State of AI Trust in 2026: Shifting to the Agentic EraNew

McKinsey's 2026 AI Trust Maturity Survey of roughly 500 organizations finds overall trust maturity improving, but strategy, governance, and agentic-AI-specific controls lag well behind. As AI systems gain autonomy, the risk shifts from systems saying the wrong thing to systems doing the wrong thing.

Key Insights

  • Average RAI (responsible AI) maturity score rose to 2.3 in 2026, up from 2.0 in 2025 — but only about 30% of organizations reach maturity level 3+ in strategy, governance, and agentic AI controls specifically
  • Nearly two-thirds of respondents cite security and risk concerns as the top barrier to scaling agentic AI — ahead of regulatory uncertainty or technical limitations
  • 74% cite inaccuracy and 72% cite cybersecurity as top AI risks; active mitigation lags risk awareness across nearly every risk category, especially IP infringement and privacy
  • AI incident frequency has held steady at roughly 8% of organizations, but confidence in incident response has declined — almost 60% of those who experienced incidents rate their response as only satisfactory or negative
  • Organizations with explicit ownership for RAI (dedicated governance roles, audit/ethics teams) average a 2.6 maturity score vs. 1.8 for those without clear accountability
  • Organizations investing $25M+ in RAI report significantly higher maturity and are far more likely to realize EBIT impact above 5%
  • Knowledge and training gaps are the #1 barrier to RAI implementation, cited by ~60% of respondents, up from ~50% last year

Key Insights

Operating Model Design for AINew

An operating model is not an org chart. It determines how decisions are made, work gets funded, outcomes are owned, and execution scales. AI stresses every part of that system and requires deliberate operating-model redesign — ownership, decision rights, governance, incentives, funding, and production control.

Key Insights

  • Without operating-model redesign, AI remains a collection of pilots and disconnected productivity tools
  • Six design components: ownership (name who owns outcomes, data, models, workflows, risk); decision rights (who approves use cases, deployment, escalation, shutdown); governance speed (risk-tiered control paths); incentives (align measures with adoption and value); funding model (move beyond pilot budgets); production control (monitoring, audit, escalation, human intervention)
  • Design for the workflow, not the model — value appears when the workflow changes and the organization can govern that change
  • Separate experimentation from production — pilots need speed, production needs ownership and accountability
  • Make risk tiering explicit so low-risk automation moves quickly and high-risk decisions receive stronger review
  • Assign accountability before deployment — if no one owns the outcome, exception path, and failure mode, the capability is not ready for production
  • Treat governance as design, not approval — governance should shape how AI operates, not appear only as a blocker at the end

Enterprise AI Hits the Wall: Privacy and Sovereignty BarriersNew

Enterprise AI is outgrowing the architectures built to support it. As privacy and sovereignty requirements tighten, data jurisdiction has become a core design constraint. Organizations redesigning early for control, locality, and security are pulling ahead; those layering AI into legacy architectures are struggling to scale.

Key Findings

  • 95%+ recognize private and sovereign AI are important; only 29% are prioritizing it concretely
  • 35% of CAIOs cite building and managing complex AI models in private/sovereign environments as their top barrier
  • Nearly 60% cite cross-border data restrictions as a major challenge
  • Only 38% report high confidence in cloud security posture — the foundation for private and sovereign AI
  • Integration complexity is the top challenge for more than 50% of organizations

Five Shifts Defining AI's Next Phase

  • AI is hitting infrastructure limits, not model limits — the constraint is control over compute, data access, security, and locality
  • Data jurisdiction is now an architectural constraint — where data lives, where models run, and how systems are governed all shift based on jurisdiction
  • Everyone sees the shift; few are acting — awareness is high, but most aren't prioritizing redesign in the near term
  • Leaders are redesigning early and creating competitive divergence — they're aligning infrastructure, governance, and operating models up-front, moving faster from pilots to scale
  • Private and sovereign AI sound independent; in practice they require tightly orchestrated ecosystems — greater control requires greater complexity and cross-partner coordination

Waypost: The Operating Framework for AI-Native TeamsNew

Legacy operating frameworks (EOS, OKRs, Scrum, Shape Up) have no governance layer for AI agents. They don't define what happens when a Post drifts, when a Contract is breached, or when the system needs to bend under pressure. Waypost fills that gap for organizations where humans and agents work together.

Key Concepts

  • Stations: Units where work happens — defined by function, outcome, or context
  • Posts: Specific, time-bounded pieces of work that move through workflow states (Unassigned → Assigned → In Motion → Complete)
  • Contracts: Agreements about what each actor (human or agent) is responsible for, what success looks like, and what triggers escalation
  • The Groundwork: Foundation elements (vision, values, context, capabilities) that all actors reference
  • The Running Order: The cadence and decision-making rhythms that keep the system functioning
  • Governance for AI: How to define what an agent should do, monitor whether it's doing it, and escalate when it's not
  • Built for hybrid human-agent teams — not human-only (Scrum) or agent-only (robot process automation)

Key Insights

  • The metaphor shifts from "building a skyscraper" (rigid, planned) to "growing a garden" (organic, evolving)
  • Emergent ability means agents accomplish tasks beyond their original design, making software continuously adaptive
  • Features become prompt configurations, not code deployments — reducing the gap between strategy and execution
